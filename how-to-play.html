How to play 4square

Rules, moves and strategy · about a five-minute read

Every level of 4square is a map whose regions are coloured with four elements: fire, water, air and earth. Neighbouring regions never share an element. One region in the middle, the hole, has no element yet. Your job is to give it one.

The goal: fill the hole

The hole has five neighbours, called its rim. At the start of a level the rim holds all four elements, so whatever you put in the hole would clash with a neighbour. You win when the rim uses only three elements: the hole then takes the fourth and the map is fully coloured.

The move: a Kempe swap

Pick any two elements, say fire and air. Start from one region and spread to every neighbour that holds one of those two elements, then their neighbours, and so on. The patch you reach is a chain: a connected group of regions that alternate between the two elements. Swapping the chain turns every fire in it into air and every air into fire.

A swap can never break the rules. Inside the chain the two elements still alternate, and every region just outside the chain holds one of the other two elements, so it cannot clash with either. This is the move Alfred Kempe invented in 1879 for his attempt at proving the Four Colour Theorem, which is why these chains are called Kempe chains.

Tracing a chain

In the default Islands style you drag (or tap tile by tile) across neighbouring regions that alternate two elements. A chain must be traced in full: every region of it glows when you have caught it all. Tap the glowing chain to swap it. Forked chains need every arm traced. Other styles show the same move in other ways: as a path through a maze, a pair of candies to switch, gears that mesh, or a door in a room.

Locks

Some chains join two rim regions holding both of the chain's elements. Swapping such a chain just exchanges those two elements around the rim, and the rim still uses all four. The game marks these chains with a padlock 🔒. When both of the classic chains Kempe would use are locked, the level is doubly locked: this is exactly the situation where Kempe's 1879 argument broke down, as Percy Heawood showed in 1890.

π, the treadmill

From the π island on, a small star in the corner of the screen tracks the rim. On a doubly locked rim one chain is forced, and the game marks it π. Swapping π never fills the hole: it moves the trouble two places round the rim and often lands on another double lock. On some maps, following π brings you back to the very same colouring after 20 swaps. Those levels teach you to look for a different chain, often one far from the rim that never touches it at all (a quiet chain).

Par, stars and coins

Every level has a par: the fewest swaps that fill the hole, computed exactly by searching all colourings of the map. Finish in par for full stars. Long chains and chains near the rim score more, and reaching a colouring you have never seen before earns a bonus. Each style pays in its own coin, and coins buy skin tickets that unlock the other styles.

Tips

  • Look for the element that appears on the rim only once. Swapping it away with a chain that does not reach the rest of the rim wins in one move.
  • If a chain is locked, try the other element of the pair, or the other copy of the repeated element.
  • On deep levels the obvious rim swaps are traps. Change something far out first so that a rim swap opens up.
  • The 🗺️ map of each level charts every colouring you have reached; it shows how big the space of colourings is and where you have been.
